Improving multi-label classification with context-aware capsule networks

Sep 25, 2018 · 3 min read

We are pleased to share that our research paper, A Context‑aware Capsule Network for Multi‑label Classification, was presented at the Workshop on Brain‑Driven Computer Vision, European Conference on Computer Vision (ECCV) 2018. The work explores how ideas inspired by the human visual system can help AI models better understand complex images containing multiple objects.

Real‑world images rarely contain just one thing. A single scene might include a chair, a desk, a window and a lamp, and the objects present often hint at what else is likely to appear. Multi‑label classification is the task of tagging an image with every object category it contains, which is useful for applications such as image search, photo organization and scene understanding. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are the standard tool for such tasks, but they have known limitations: pooling creates a trade‑off between preserving spatial information and being invariant to transformations, and CNNs handle rotation only to a limited extent. Capsule Networks (CapsNets), introduced by Geoffrey Hinton and his collaborators in 2017, take a different approach. Instead of a single number, each capsule outputs a vector that describes an object or feature along with its properties, such as its orientation or position. However, the original CapsNet did not explicitly model the structured relationships between the entities it detects, which is important for complex scenes.

To address this, we proposed a context‑aware Capsule Network with three improvements. First, we made the initial routing weights, which control how information flows between capsule layers, trainable instead of equal, so they depend on the low‑level features. This helped the network converge faster, with validation performance stabilizing after about 9 epochs instead of 15. Second, we added an end‑to‑end trainable, densely connected Conditional Random Field (CRF) that models dependencies between the properties predicted for different object categories, helping the network capture the context of neighbouring objects. Third, we introduced a correlation module, based on a property of the Cholesky transformation, that learns a dataset‑wide priority scheme for how much each low‑level capsule’s prediction should count towards each object category. On ADE20K, a challenging scene dataset with 150 object categories, our architecture improved mean average precision (mAP) from 42.38 to 56.71 compared with the original Capsule Network.

This research suggests that incorporating contextual relationships into capsule network architectures can improve the interpretation of complex visual scenes. By enabling models to consider not only individual visual features but also how they relate to one another, this approach offers a promising direction for multi‑label image classification and, more broadly, for structured prediction tasks in computer vision. The paper was authored by Sameera Ramasinghe, who is affiliated with ConscientAI and is also a PhD student at the Australian National University (ANU), Australia, C.D. Athuraliya from ConscientAI, and Salman H. Khan from ANU.
